Your accounts run dry. Your work doesn’t.

how it works

Connect. Point. Keep working.

Every account has a ceiling, and you meet it mid-run. Apiary watches each account’s limits and spends them in the order that keeps you working.

01

Connect

Sign in and connect what you have: Claude plans, Anthropic API keys, Codex, Kimi, a model on your own GPU. Apiary checks each one’s limits, and keeps checking.

02

Point

Point your agents at Apiary, once. They ask for a class (fast, capable or frontier) instead of a model, and you decide what each class means and what it falls back to.

03

Keep working

Before an account runs out, the next request goes to another, mid-run, so nothing comes back half-finished. The status line stays quiet until you’re low, and Apiary can tell you when you’ll next run out.

Join the hive

apiary.wtf runs Apiary for invited members. Sign in with Google or GitHub, connect your accounts on the web, and point your agents at it. Invite-only for now: join the waggle list.

Run your own

The code is public. Two small services in Docker Compose, on your own machine, and your keys never leave it. Start with the README.

What it keeps

Your prompts and code fly through and aren’t kept. Apiary logs each trip (when, which account, how many tokens), and your keys are locked. The privacy page says it all in plain words.

the keepers

Meet your
fellow keepers

Everyone here keeps more than one hive. Apiary makes each keeper’s own hives work as one.

  1. Marigold Thrice

    three hives, all hers

    “One hive was never enough for me, so now I keep three. The trouble used to come mid-job: a hive would run dry, the whole swarm would down tools, and when the flow came back they’d swear they had finished. They had not finished. Now when one gets low the workers slip next door and carry on. I hear nothing until all three are nearly empty, and it tells me the day that will be.”

  2. Basil Overbee

    runs a tight colony

    “Every worker here has a role. Scouts plan, builders build, inspectors check the comb, and I redo the rota most mornings, depending on who’s having a good week. Nothing touches my rota. I wanted one small thing: when my best forager’s hive runs low, she carries on from my other hive, and nobody swaps her for someone cheaper. Both hives are mine. It doesn’t go near anyone else’s.”

  3. The Ten Hive Society

    not beekeepers by trade

    “None of us keep bees for a living. We just found we couldn’t get our work done without them, so we bought a hive, then another, and somehow ended up with ten. For months we’d work one until it ran dry, pick everything up, carry it to the next and start again, days at a stretch. The honey bill was remarkable. Now it’s one big hive as far as anyone here can tell, and we can finally see where the honey goes.”

  4. Penny Combsworth

    counts every drop

    “The queen does the thinking. The workers do the building, and they do it perfectly well on sugar water. I’m not paying royal-jelly prices for a job a worker can do, and some jobs never leave my own garden at all. Before I let it near a hive I asked who keeps the key to the honey house. The answer is on the privacy page, in plain words. I read the whole thing. I’ve never done that before.”

All hives shown are honey-cooled.

what comes next

Next: share the idle.

Most accounts sit idle all week, then run out in a crunch, and everyone’s crunch comes at a different time. Once the hive has its keepers, Apiary will let members pool capacity that sits idle, only where a provider’s terms allow it, and draw on the pool when it’s their turn to burst. If the pool goes away, you’re back to exactly your own accounts. Read the business plan →
